Current Projects

The MHP conducts collaborative research across several interrelated themes, all drawing on our 35+ year longitudinal dataset and ongoing field monitoring.

01

Urbanization & Cognitive Evolution

Lead: Dr. Lily Johnson-Ulrich — Hunter College CUNY

As human settlements expand around wildlife habitats, some hyena populations are increasingly exposed to urban environments while others remain in wild areas. This project uses this variation as a natural experiment to test the Exaptation Model of cognitive evolution: the hypothesis that cognitive traits evolved in one context can be co-opted to solve novel problems in radically different environments.

We compare spotted hyenas across an urbanization gradient in the Maasai Mara region, measuring neophilia/neophobia (boldness toward novel objects), problem-solving ability, and individual variation in behavior. Camera traps with controlled novel object arrays allow behavioral assessment at scale across multiple individuals simultaneously.

The project links cognitive and behavioral measurements to fitness outcomes tracked through our long-term database, directly testing whether urban-associated cognitive traits confer advantages in changing environments.

02

Social Networks & Early-Life Effects

Lead: Dr. Eli Strauss — Max Planck Institute of Animal Behavior

Spotted hyenas live in large, complex societies with fluid subgroup structure and stable dominance hierarchies. This project investigates how the social environment during early life shapes adult social behavior, stress physiology, and lifetime fitness.

Using detailed social network analysis applied to decades of behavioral observation data, we quantify each individual’s social integration, dominance rank, and relationship quality across their lifetime, linking these to long-term outcomes including survival, reproductive success, and stress hormone concentrations from non-invasive fecal samples.

Recent work has combined long-term behavioral data with molecular genetic analysis of gene expression patterns, revealing lasting epigenetic effects of early social experience — findings with broad relevance across mammals, including humans.

03

GPS Collaring & Movement Ecology

Collaborative — Multiple MHP Directors

GPS collaring of individual hyenas enables fine-scale tracking of movement, ranging behavior, and space use across the Maasai Mara landscape. This project combines GPS data with behavioral observations and environmental covariates to understand how hyenas navigate a complex, human-modified landscape.

Key questions: How do clan territory boundaries shift over time? How do individuals respond to tourists, livestock, and human settlements? How does space use relate to social rank, age, sex, and reproductive state? And critically — do urban-exposed hyenas move differently than those in wilder areas?

04

Long-Term Demography & Clan Monitoring

Ongoing baseline — All MHP Directors

The foundation of all MHP research is continuous baseline monitoring of the Serena North and Talek clans. Every day, our field team conducts observation sessions to record clan composition, individual identities, behavioral interactions, and life events (births, deaths, rank changes, dispersal).

This monitoring produces the longitudinal dataset that makes all other MHP research possible. Baseline monitoring also serves a direct conservation function: our data track population trends, document threats, and provide the evidence base for conservation recommendations to park authorities and government agencies.
