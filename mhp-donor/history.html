Our History

Over 35 years of continuous field research in Kenya’s Maasai Mara Reserve.

The Beginning

Founded in 1988

The Mara Hyena Project was founded by Dr. Kay Holekamp of Michigan State University in 1988, initially as a study of maternal effects on offspring behavior in spotted hyenas. What began as a focused field study grew into one of the longest continuously running wildlife studies in Africa.

From the outset, the project established a commitment to individual-level data collection — identifying every hyena in the study clans by spot pattern and ear notches, and recording detailed behavioral observations on each animal from birth through adulthood. This longitudinal approach is what makes the MHP dataset uniquely powerful.

1988

Project Founded

Dr. Kay Holekamp begins the study in the Maasai Mara, initially focusing on the Talek clan. The project establishes its first field camp and begins the painstaking work of identifying individual hyenas.

Early 1990s

Expansion to Serena

The project expands to include a second study clan near Serena Lodge, providing a spatial comparison and doubling the scope of longitudinal data collection.

1990s–2000s

Building the Database

Decades of daily observation accumulate into a comprehensive relational database tracking individual life histories, social relationships, reproductive success, and survival. The MHP database becomes one of the most detailed long-term animal datasets in existence.

2000s

Community Integration

The MHP deepens its relationship with Maasai communities surrounding the reserve, establishing free human-hyena conflict consulting services and educational programs for local schools.

2010s

Expanding Research Questions

The project broadens its scientific scope to include cognition, endocrinology, epigenetics, and urbanization — drawing on the long-term dataset to answer increasingly sophisticated questions.

2018

30th Anniversary

The MHP marks 30 years of continuous operation, with over 150 peer-reviewed publications and a team of Co-Directors who trained with the project as students.

Present

35+ Years and Counting

The MHP continues daily monitoring of the Serena North and Talek clans, with an expanded team of Co-Directors, dedicated Kenyan field staff, and new research initiatives on cognition, urbanization, and conservation.

A Legacy Built by Many

Hundreds of students, field assistants, and collaborators have contributed to the MHP over the decades. The project’s continuity is a testament to the commitment of a remarkable team spread across two continents.
