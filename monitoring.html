Ecological Monitoring

Conservation depends on knowing what is actually happening on the ground. Our long-term monitoring turns daily field research into the evidence managers and communities need to protect hyenas and the ecosystem they anchor.

Why We Monitor

Long-Term Data, Direct Conservation Value

Since 1988, the MHP has recorded not only spotted hyenas but the ecosystem around them: the predators they compete with, the prey they depend on, the weather that shapes the landscape, and the ways hyenas and people share the same ground. Individually, each of these monitoring streams answers a scientific question. Together, they form an early-warning system, showing when and where hyena populations come under pressure, and pointing to the interventions most likely to help.

The five programs below are the backbone of that work.

What We Track

Five Monitoring Programs

Predator & Prey Monitoring

We record the wider carnivore and herbivore community alongside hyenas, from lions and leopards to the resident and migratory ungulates hyenas rely on. Tracking how prey availability and competitor densities shift over time shows how changes elsewhere in the ecosystem ripple through to hyenas, and helps detect decline before it becomes irreversible.

Climate Monitoring

Rainfall and temperature drive grass growth, the movement of migratory herds, and ultimately the food available to hyenas. Our long-term climate records let us link environmental variation to hyena reproduction, survival, and behavior, and to anticipate how a changing climate may reshape the Mara.

Hyena Demography & Survival

Because every individual in our study clans is known and followed from birth, we can measure birth rates, survival, dispersal, and cause of death with rare precision. These data reveal whether populations are stable, growing, or declining, and identify which threats are driving the change.

Livestock Depredation

When hyenas kill livestock, the result is often retaliatory killing that can wipe out entire clans. We document depredation events and the conflict they generate, work that underpins efforts to reduce losses for herders and to design interventions that protect both livelihoods and hyenas.

Space Use & Movement (GPS Collars)

GPS collars let us follow individual hyenas as they move across the landscape, inside and outside the protected area. Knowing where hyenas range, how far they travel, and when they cross into community and livestock areas is essential for predicting conflict hotspots and guiding land-use and conservation planning.

Monitoring That Informs Action

Every one of these programs feeds back into conservation on the ground, from reducing human-hyena conflict to shaping how the Mara is managed. Sustaining this work over decades is what makes it valuable, and it depends on the support of funders, donors, and Kenyan partners.
