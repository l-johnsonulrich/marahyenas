Hyena Behavior

An introduction to the social lives of spotted hyenas, drawn from nearly four decades of research in the Maasai Mara.

Society

Social Structure

Spotted hyenas live in matrilineal societies called clans, which can include up to 130 individuals in the prey-rich Maasai Mara. Clans are organized as fission-fusion societies, meaning clan members rarely gather all together; instead, individuals associate in smaller subgroups that shift in composition throughout the day.

Within a clan, females are socially dominant to all immigrant males, and rank is inherited from the mother in a system structurally similar to that of many cercopithecine primates. A cub's adult social position is largely determined before it is even weaned, with profound consequences for lifetime reproductive success, access to food, and survival.

Communication

Vocalizations & Signals

Spotted hyenas have one of the most diverse vocal repertoires of any terrestrial carnivore. The long-distance whoop carries for kilometers and is individually distinctive, allowing clan members to recognize one another across the savanna and to coordinate movement. Giggles, groans, grunts, lows, and rumbles are used in close-range social interactions, with acoustic structure encoding information about the caller's identity, dominance rank, and motivational state.

Hyenas also communicate through scent marking with paste from their anal glands, deposited along clan territory boundaries and at communal latrines. Visual signals, including tail position, ear orientation, and the conspicuous "meeting ceremony" in which clan members investigate one another's erect phalluses, mediate close-range social interactions and reinforce social bonds.

Foraging

Hunting & Feeding

Despite a longstanding reputation as scavengers, spotted hyenas obtain most of their food by hunting. In the Maasai Mara, hyenas regularly take down adult wildebeest, zebra, and Thomson's gazelle, often by themselves or in small coordinated groups. Larger prey such as Cape buffalo are taken cooperatively by multiple hunters.

Hyenas have the most powerful jaws of any African carnivore relative to their size, enabling them to crush bones and extract marrow that other predators cannot access. At a kill, social rank strongly predicts feeding priority: dominant females and their offspring eat first, and feeding bouts are often punctuated by intense competition both within the clan and with lions, which routinely usurp hyena kills.

Reproduction

Mating & Maternal Care

Female spotted hyenas have a unique reproductive anatomy: their external genitalia are masculinized, with a fully erectile pseudopenis through which they urinate, copulate, and give birth. This unusual morphology is associated with high prenatal androgen exposure but does not appear to reflect male-like sexual behavior or aggression in adults.

Litters typically consist of one or two cubs, born at a remote natal den before being moved to a clan-wide communal den within a few weeks. Cubs are born with eyes open and teeth erupted, and same-sex twin siblings sometimes engage in intense aggression within the first days of life. Maternal investment is exceptionally long for a carnivore: cubs nurse for up to 18 months and remain dependent on their mothers for protection and feeding opportunities well past weaning.

Cognition

Intelligence & Problem-Solving

Spotted hyenas are among the most cognitively flexible carnivores studied to date. They readily solve novel physical problems, learn from observing conspecifics, and adjust their behavior to changing social conditions. Experimental work using puzzle boxes has shown substantial individual variation in innovation and persistence, with bolder and more exploratory hyenas typically more successful at extracting hidden food rewards.

Hyena social cognition is similarly sophisticated. Individuals recognize the kinship and rank relationships of other clan members, recruit specific allies during conflicts, and modify their behavior depending on which clan members are nearby. This capacity for tracking complex social information has made spotted hyenas an important model species for testing hypotheses about the evolution of mammalian intelligence.

Daily Life

Activity & Movement

Spotted hyenas are primarily nocturnal and crepuscular, with peak activity in the hours around dawn and dusk. During the heat of the day, clan members rest in dense vegetation, in mud wallows, or at communal dens. Hyenas may travel 20 kilometers or more in a single night while patrolling territory boundaries, hunting, scavenging, or visiting clan members at the den.

Clan territories in the Maasai Mara are defended collectively against neighboring clans, with confrontations between clans sometimes involving 20 or more individuals on each side. Territorial defense, like much of hyena social life, depends heavily on coalitions: which clan members show up, and who their allies are, often determines the outcome.

The Mara Hyena Project has monitored spotted hyena clans in Kenya’s Maasai Mara since 1988.
